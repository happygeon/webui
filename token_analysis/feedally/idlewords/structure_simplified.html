The Shape of a Mars Mission

This post is the second in a series. Read part one here.

“From a mathematics and trajectory standpoint... - John Aaron [1]

Unlike the Moon, Mars has a limited travel window every 26 months, making missions complex.

Using chemical rockets, there are two mission types:

  • Long Stay: 6 months to Mars, 17 months stay, 6 months back (~1000 days total).
  • Short Stay: 6 months to Mars, 30-90 days stay, 400 days back (~650 days total).

Both missions are long, exceeding previous space flight records, and pose significant challenges.

The length of the missions is determined by planetary motion, making technological improvements difficult.

Delta-v requirements for short and long stay missions show significant challenges.

I. No escape, no rescue

A Mars trip will be a commitment with no option for quick return, unlike previous missions.

If issues arise, the crew may have to wait years for rescue, similar to early Antarctic explorers.

Delta-v requirements for returning to Earth from various points in a long-stay Mars mission.

This no-abort condition increases risk aversion in mission design.

II. Reliability

The ISS is not considered highly reliable, requiring constant repairs and replacements.

Defective equipment must be fixed by the crew during a Mars mission.

Life support systems must be highly reliable, as failures can have severe consequences.

III. Autonomy

Mars missions will require crew autonomy due to communication delays with Earth.

Autonomy allows astronauts to manage systems without ground control, but increases workload.

As distance increases, communication delays will hinder real-time support from Earth.

IV. Analysis

Mars missions will need onboard analysis capabilities for air and water samples.

Automated systems must be developed for biochemical assays in space.

V. Automation

Automation will be essential to reduce workload on a Mars mission.

Automation introduces complexity, requiring thorough testing before missions.

Implications

Mars missions will be the most complex human artifacts ever built, combining many systems.

Preparing for Mars will be a lengthy process, requiring extensive testing and development.

Choosing a profile

The long-stay profile is preferred for exploration, while the short-stay profile is riskier.

Long Stay

The long stay profile offers simplicity but poses challenges for activities on Mars.

Short Stay

The short stay profile allows quick missions but complicates the return journey.

Orbit or Land?

Deciding whether to land or orbit Mars is crucial, with pros and cons for each approach.

NASA has debated the best approach for years, weighing safety and mission goals.

Key questions remain about human health in partial gravity and radiation risks.

Understanding these risks is essential for mission design and crew safety.

The complexity of Mars missions requires careful planning and consideration of risks.

My goal is to encourage realistic views on Mars exploration and its challenges.

There is a divide in space exploration between ambitious missions and those focused on human presence.

We can either explore aggressively with robots or slowly build capacity for human landings.
